Land disturbance, grading & stormwaterThe costs that hit before a single board is cut — and the most common reason a building permit gets held up.

Core — call sheetHigh costldp_required

“Do I need a land disturbance, grading, or site development permit before you'll issue the building permit?”

In many jurisdictions this is a separate permit with its own application, drawings, fees, and review cycle — and the building permit will not be issued until it clears. Homeowners routinely discover it after signing a contract that never mentioned it.

Then ask the builderIf a land disturbance permit is required here, is the application, the drawings, and the fee in your bid — or mine to pay?
Yes — required before the building permit

Get three things in writing from your builder before you sign. First, who prepares and submits the application. Second, who pays for the required drawings — this is the line most often left out, and it is commonly an owner cost. Third, whether their construction schedule accounts for the review cycle, which typically runs weeks rather than days. Ask for this as a named line item in the contract, not a verbal assurance. If your builder says it's 'included in site work,' ask them to show you the dollar amount inside that number.

Only above a certain lot size or disturbed area

Get the exact threshold in writing from the county, then find out how much of your lot the build will actually disturb — house, driveway, septic field, staging area, and any laydown or spoil area all count toward it, which is what pushes projects over the line unexpectedly. Ask your builder for their disturbed-acreage figure and compare it to the threshold. If you're anywhere near it, budget as if the permit is required. Ask specifically what happens if the number is exceeded mid-build.

No separate permit required

Good — that's one fewer gate. Still confirm two things with your builder: that no erosion control or grading work is being billed to you as a change order later, and that they aren't assuming a permit cost in their number that no longer applies. If a site or grading allowance appears in the bid, ask what it covers now that this permit isn't in play.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

This is worth one more call — it's among the most expensive answers on this list. Ask for the county's Development Services, Land Development, or Engineering department rather than the general building permit desk; the front counter often doesn't handle site permits. If you can't get through, ask your builder to tell you in writing whether one is required and to name the department they confirmed it with.

Core — call sheetHigh costsite_plan_seal

“Does my site plan have to be prepared and sealed by a licensed surveyor or engineer — and does it have to show existing and proposed grades, or cut and fill?”

A seal means a licensed professional has to be hired. This is a third-party cost the county requires but does not charge you for, so it never appears on a fee schedule — and it is one of the most common four-figure surprises in residential construction.

Then ask the builderIs a sealed site plan included in your bid, and if so, whose surveyor or engineer is preparing it?
Yes — must be sealed by a licensed professional

This is a real cost and it is very often the owner's. Ask your builder directly whether a sealed site plan is inside their number, and if they say yes, ask for the dollar amount and the name of the firm. If it isn't included, get quotes from two licensed surveyors or civil engineers in that county before you sign, so you're negotiating with a real number rather than an estimate. Also ask the county what level of detail the seal has to cover — a simple plot plan and a full grading plan with cut and fill staging are very different prices.

Only in certain cases (slope, size, or disturbed area)

Find out exactly what triggers it and whether your lot crosses that line. Slope is the usual trigger, and it's the one homeowners misjudge — a lot that looks gently rolling can carry enough fall to trigger a full grading plan. Ask your builder which side of the trigger they assumed when they priced the job, and get that assumption written into the contract so it's clear who absorbs the cost if it turns out to be wrong.

No — a simple site plan is acceptable

Confirm what they will accept, and get it in writing if you can — some offices accept a hand-drawn plot plan and others want a scaled drawing even without a seal. Then confirm with your builder that they're producing it at no additional charge. If the county later asks for more detail than expected, you want the contract to already say who handles it.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask this one alongside the land disturbance question — the same department usually answers both, and the two together account for most pre-construction permitting cost. In the meantime, ask your builder to state in writing whether their bid includes a sealed site plan. Their answer is useful either way: if they don't know, that tells you something about how the rest of the site work was priced.

ExtendedHigh coststormwater_detention

“Does a single-family lot here require stormwater management — detention, retention, or a drainage study?”

Stormwater requirements on a single lot are uncommon but expensive when they apply, and they can consume usable yard area as well as budget.

Then ask the builderIs any stormwater management structure in your site number?
Yes — required

Ask what form it takes and who has to design it, since an engineered detention facility is a different cost from a simple swale. Get the requirement in writing and take it to your builder for a separate line item. Also ask where it has to sit on the lot, because it consumes area you may have been counting on for yard, drive, or septic.

Only above a size or impervious-area threshold

Get the threshold and ask your builder for their total impervious area — roof, driveway, walks, patios — and compare. Homeowners routinely underestimate this because driveways and patios add up faster than expected. If you're near the line, ask what a change in scope later would trigger.

Not required for a single lot

Confirm there's no separate drainage or grading requirement that accomplishes the same thing under a different name. Then make sure your builder's site number still includes positive drainage away from the house and any needed swales, since that's good practice regardless of what's required.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask this alongside the land disturbance question — same department, and the two answers usually come together. It's lower priority than the core list unless your lot is flat, low, or has water crossing it.

ExtendedModerate costesc_maintenance

“If erosion and sediment control is required, who inspects it, how often, and how long must it be maintained?”

Erosion control isn't just an install cost — it's an ongoing maintenance obligation with inspections and potential fines, and the duration is often longer than the build.

Then ask the builderDoes your bid include erosion control maintenance for the full required duration, or just the initial installation?
Required with ongoing inspections

Ask how long the obligation runs and what triggers release — usually permanent stabilization such as established grass. Then ask your builder specifically whether their number covers maintenance for that whole period or just the initial install, since that gap is common. Also ask who is responsible for any fines if an inspection fails.

Only if a land disturbance permit is required

Then this rides on your land disturbance answer. If that permit applies, come back to this question and get the maintenance duration, because it's the part most often missing from a bid. If it doesn't apply, note it and move on.

Not required

Confirm with your builder that basic silt fencing and stabilization are still in their scope as good practice, particularly if your lot slopes toward a road, a neighbor, or water. Not being required doesn't make the erosion stop.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Lower priority — ask it if the land disturbance answer came back yes, and skip it otherwise. It's a maintenance and fine exposure more than a large upfront cost.

ExtendedModerate costtree_ordinance

“Is there a tree ordinance — a survey requirement, a removal permit, protection fencing, or replacement or recompense fees?”

Tree ordinances can require a surveyed tree inventory before clearing and charge replacement fees for removals, both of which are owner costs that never appear on a fee schedule.

Then ask the builderDoes your clearing number account for any tree survey, protection, or replacement requirements?
Yes — tree requirements apply

Ask exactly what's required before clearing — an inventory by a licensed professional, protective fencing, or a permit — and what the replacement or recompense formula is. Take it to your builder and ask whether clearing was priced with these in mind. Ask before any trees come down, because removing a protected tree first and asking later is the expensive order of operations.

Only for certain species or sizes

Get the species and size thresholds in writing and ask whether anyone has walked your lot against them. If your lot is wooded, this is worth resolving before clearing starts rather than after. Ask your builder who is responsible if a protected tree is removed by mistake.

No tree ordinance

Then clearing is a straightforward scope question. Confirm with your builder what area their clearing number covers and what happens to the debris — burning, chipping, and hauling off are three different costs, and hauling is the expensive one.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask the planning or zoning department if your lot is wooded; skip it if the lot is already clear. It's usually a modest cost but it has to be resolved before clearing rather than after.

ExtendedModerate costgeotech

“Do you require a soils or geotechnical report for a residential foundation?”

Where required, this is a licensed third-party cost, and its findings can change the foundation design itself — which is a far larger number than the report.

Then ask the builderIs a soils report required here, and if the findings require a different foundation, how is that handled in our contract?
Yes — required

Ask whether it's required at permit application or before foundation inspection, then confirm with your builder whether it's in their bid and who selects the engineer. More importantly, ask what happens to your price if the report calls for a deeper footing, engineered fill, or piers — get that answer in writing before signing, because the report is cheap and its consequences are not.

Only in certain areas or conditions

Find out what triggers it and whether your parcel is in that category. If you're near the line, treat the report as likely and ask your builder to state their foundation assumptions in writing so you can tell what a different finding would change.

Not required

Not being required doesn't mean the soil is good. Ask your builder what they assumed about soil conditions and what your contract says about rock, unsuitable soil, or undocumented fill — this is one of the most common change order categories in all of residential construction, and the contract language matters more than the report.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Lower priority unless your lot is steep, was previously filled, or sits near water. Regardless of the answer, ask your builder how unsuitable soil and rock are handled in the contract — that question is worth asking everywhere.

Septic, well, water & sewerThe single largest source of five-figure surprises on rural and semi-rural lots.

Core — call sheetVery high costseptic_or_sewer

“Is this lot on public sewer or septic — and if septic, does the health department have to approve the system before you'll issue the building permit?”

Septic approval is a separate agency, a separate timeline, and a separate cost, and it commonly gates the building permit. A failed or marginal soil result can force an engineered or advanced treatment system that costs several times a conventional one.

Then ask the builderWhat septic system did you price, and what happens to my price if the soil test requires an engineered or alternative system?
Septic — health department approval required first

Do not sign a fixed-price contract until you know the soil result. Ask your builder three questions in writing: what type and size of system their number assumes, what the price becomes if an engineered or alternative system is required, and who pays for the soil evaluation itself. This is the single largest swing on a rural lot, and 'we'll handle it' is not an answer you can budget against. If the soil test hasn't been done yet, ask whether the contract can make the septic line an open allowance with a written unit price rather than a lump sum.

Public sewer available

Then your exposure moves to connection cost rather than system cost. Ask the utility what the tap, capacity, and connection fees total, and how far the sewer main is from your building site — the lateral run from the main to the house is often the owner's cost and scales with distance. Confirm with your builder whether their bid includes the tap fee, the lateral, or neither. Also ask whether the connection requires a road cut, which carries its own permit and restoration cost.

Depends on the lot / still being determined

Treat this as unresolved and price it as a risk. Ask the county what determines which way it goes and when that determination gets made. Until it's settled, ask your builder to show both scenarios — what the bid looks like on sewer and what it looks like on septic — so you can see the spread before you commit. A builder who can only show you one number is carrying the other one as a change order.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

This one is worth chasing down before anything else on the list. The septic or sewer question is usually answered by the county health department or environmental health office rather than the building department, so ask to be transferred. If your lot is rural and unbuilt, assume septic and ask your builder what they assumed until you can confirm.

Core — call sheetVery high costperc_soil_test

“Who performs the soil or percolation evaluation, what does it cost, and what are my options if the lot doesn't pass for a conventional system?”

A marginal soil result doesn't usually stop a build — it upgrades the system, and the upgrade is where the money is. Homeowners who only learn this after signing absorb it as a change order.

Then ask the builderHas the soil evaluation been done on this lot, and if not, is your septic number an allowance or a fixed price?
Already done — conventional system approved

Get a copy of the approval and the approved system size in writing, and make sure the system your builder priced matches what was approved — bedroom count drives sizing, so if the plan changed after the evaluation, the approval may not match the house. Confirm the approval's expiration date; some are time-limited and have to be renewed if you don't break ground in time.

Not done yet / results pending

Until this comes back, your septic number is a guess no matter how confidently it's presented. Ask your builder to write the septic line as an allowance with a stated basis — system type, size, and unit cost — rather than a lump sum, so you can see exactly what a different result changes. Ask what their price becomes for the two most likely upgrade paths. If a builder resists putting that in writing, that reluctance is itself information.

Failed conventional / engineered system required

Get the health department's written determination and take it straight to your builder before signing anything. Ask for a revised, itemized septic number based on the actual required system, and get quotes from two septic contractors independently so you can check it. Also ask whether the required system changes the site layout — some advanced systems need substantially more area, which can move the house, the driveway, or the grading plan, and each of those has its own cost.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Call the county environmental health or health department directly rather than the building office. Ask two things: whether an evaluation exists on record for your parcel, and what the process and cost is if it doesn't. Meanwhile, ask your builder in writing what system type and size their bid assumes — you need that number on record before the test comes back, not after.

Core — call sheetHigh costtap_capacity_fees

“What are the water and sewer tap, meter, and capacity fees for a new home at this address, and how far is the connection point from my building site?”

Connection fees are set by the utility, not the builder, and the run from the main to the house is usually the owner's cost and scales with distance. Both are commonly outside a base bid.

Then ask the builderDoes your bid include the water tap, the meter, and the service line run from the main to the house?
Public water available — fees quoted

Write down each fee separately: tap, meter, capacity or system development charge. Then ask your builder which of those their bid covers and who runs the service line from the meter to the house. That line is frequently the owner's, priced by the foot, and on a deep lot it's a meaningful number. Also ask whether the connection requires cutting a road, which usually carries a separate permit and a restoration requirement.

No public water — well required

Ask the county whether a well permit is required, what testing is required before the certificate of occupancy, and whether there are setback requirements between the well and the septic field that could affect your site layout. Then get well drilling quotes independently — well cost is driven by depth, which varies by area and cannot be known precisely in advance, so ask your builder whether their number is a fixed price or an allowance and what the per-foot rate is beyond it.

Available but the main is some distance away

Get the distance in feet and ask the utility whether extending the main is your cost, theirs, or shared — this is the answer that matters and it varies enormously. If it's yours, get it priced before you sign, because a main extension can rival the cost of a well. Ask whether any neighbors are also connecting, since some utilities share extension cost across connections.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

The water utility is usually a separate authority from the county, so this may be a different phone number than the rest of your list. Ask the building department who provides water at your address, then call them. Until then, ask your builder in writing what they assumed for water source and connection cost.

Driveway, access & addressingUsually small money, but they gate the permit and the inspections.

Core — call sheetModerate costdriveway_permit

“Do I need a driveway, culvert, or right-of-way encroachment permit — and is my road county-maintained or state-maintained?”

Which authority owns the road determines who issues the permit and what they require. A state highway connection is a different process, timeline, and specification than a county road, and the culvert and apron are often owner costs.

Then ask the builderIs the driveway permit, the culvert, and the apron in your bid?
Yes — permit required

Ask who issues it and what they specify — culvert size, apron material, sight distance, and drainage requirements all get dictated rather than chosen. Then confirm with your builder whether the permit, the culvert pipe, and the apron are in their number or in a site allowance. If it's in an allowance, ask what happens when the county specifies something more expensive than the allowance assumed, because you don't get to negotiate that specification.

Depends on the road / handled by the state DOT

Find out which agency owns your road frontage and go directly to them — a state route connection typically has a longer timeline and tighter specifications than a county road, and the review can run weeks. Ask about the timeline specifically, and make sure your builder's schedule reflects it, since the driveway is often needed for construction access before the house starts.

No permit required

Confirm there's no culvert or drainage requirement even without a permit, since some jurisdictions specify one without requiring a formal application. Then check what your builder priced for the driveway — length, base, and surface — against the actual distance from the road to your house, because driveway is one of the most commonly under-scoped site lines.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Start by finding out who maintains your road — the county building or public works department can tell you, and that determines who you call next. This one is usually low cost but it gates construction access, so an unanswered driveway permit can delay a start date even when the money is small.

ExtendedLow costaddressing_911

“Do I need a 911 address assigned before you'll issue the permit, and who requests it?”

Small money, but it's a gate — permits and utility accounts often can't be opened without an assigned address, and it can take days to weeks.

Then ask the builderHas the address been assigned for this lot, and who's handling it?
Required before permit

Find out who requests it and how long it takes, then make sure someone actually starts it — this is a classic item that everyone assumes the other party handled. Confirm with your builder in writing who owns this step. It's small, and it will hold up your permit if it's missed.

Not required in advance

Note it and move on. You'll still need an address eventually for utilities and deliveries, so ask when in the process it typically gets assigned.

Only for new lots or subdivisions

Find out whether your lot already has an assigned address — a newly split or subdivided parcel usually won't. If it doesn't, treat it as required and confirm who's requesting it.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Very low cost, easy to catch later. Ask it when you next call, or simply ask your builder to confirm the lot has an assigned address before permit submittal.

Permit fees, impact fees & plan reviewRarely itemized in a bid, and almost never small.

Core — call sheetVery high costimpact_fees

“Are there impact fees, development fees, or capacity fees on a new single-family home here that are separate from the building permit fee — for schools, parks, roads, fire, or utilities?”

Impact fees are separate from permit fees, are frequently far larger, and are almost never carried in a builder's base bid. They're also invisible on most fee schedules because they're administered by a different department.

Then ask the builderAre impact fees included in your permit allowance, and what number did you use?
Yes — impact fees apply

Ask the county for the total dollar figure for a new single-family home at your address, in writing if they'll give it, and ask when it's due — some are collected at permit issuance and some at certificate of occupancy, which changes your cash flow. Then compare that number to whatever permit allowance is in your bid. If the bid carries a single 'permits' line, ask your builder to break it into permit fee, impact fees, and utility connection fees, because those are three different things and a single lump number usually only covers the first.

No impact fees here

Confirm that this covers all the departments — sometimes a county has no general impact fee but a water or fire authority charges its own capacity fee separately. Ask whether any other agency collects a fee at permit issuance. Then check what permit allowance your builder carried, since a number set for a higher-fee jurisdiction should come back to you rather than quietly staying in the price.

Depends on the district or utility provider

Get the name of every agency that collects a fee at your address — county, city, water authority, fire district, school district — and call each one, because they rarely know each other's numbers. Add them up yourself. Then hand the total to your builder and ask which portion their bid covers. This is tedious and it is exactly where several thousand dollars hides.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Worth a second call. Ask specifically for 'impact fees' or 'development impact fees' by name — if you ask about 'permit fees' you'll often be quoted only the building permit itself and told nothing about the rest. Until you have a number, treat any 'permits' line in your bid as unverified.

Core — call sheetModerate costpermit_fee_basis

“How is the building permit fee calculated here — flat, per square foot, or a percentage of construction valuation — and if it's valuation-based, what valuation do you use?”

Valuation-based fees are set by the jurisdiction's own cost tables, not your contract price, so the fee can be higher than a builder's assumption. It also determines whether the number in your bid is a real quote or a placeholder.

Then ask the builderWhat permit fee figure is in your bid, and is it a quoted number or an estimate?
Got a specific figure or formula

Compare it against whatever permit line is in your bid. If the bid's number is lower, ask your builder to correct it rather than absorbing the difference later as a change order. If the bid has no permit line at all, that means permits are your cost — confirm that in writing, and make sure you know the full list of what you're paying, not just the building permit itself.

Depends on valuation or square footage

Ask what valuation figure they'd use for a home of your size and finish level, since jurisdictions commonly use their own cost tables rather than your contract price. Get the resulting fee estimate and compare it to the bid. If the bid's permit allowance was set for a different jurisdiction or an older fee schedule, this is the moment to catch it.

They wouldn't quote until application

That's common. Ask for the published fee schedule instead — most jurisdictions have one online or will send it — and work out the approximate figure yourself. Then ask your builder to write the permit line as a pass-through at actual cost rather than a fixed allowance, so neither of you is guessing and no one profits from the estimate being wrong.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

This is usually the easiest question on the list to get answered — the permit counter handles it directly and most jurisdictions publish a fee schedule. Worth one more call, mainly because it's the anchor for checking every other fee number in your bid.

ExtendedModerate costplan_review

“How long does plan review take, is it done in-house or by a third party, and is there a fee for resubmittal if corrections are required?”

Review time is schedule, and schedule is carrying cost. Resubmittal fees and third-party review charges are separate from the permit fee and rarely anticipated.

Then ask the builderHow long did you assume for permitting in the schedule, and who pays for resubmittals?
Got a timeline and fee structure

Compare the review timeline against your builder's schedule and your financing. If the schedule assumes a faster turnaround than the county quotes, ask them to correct it now — an optimistic permit assumption pushes every date behind it. Also confirm who absorbs resubmittal fees if corrections come back on the builder's drawings.

Varies with volume or season

Ask what the current wait actually is rather than the published target, and ask when their busy season falls. Build the realistic number into your expectations and ask your builder to do the same. If you're financing, an extra month of review is a real cost to you and worth knowing before you set a closing date.

Over the counter / very fast

Good news for your schedule. Still ask whether that applies to the building permit only or to the site and septic permits as well, since those often run on separate and slower tracks and are the ones that gate the start.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Low priority for cost, moderate for schedule. Ask it whenever you next reach the permit desk, and in the meantime ask your builder what permitting duration their schedule assumes.

Code, testing & certificate of occupancyWhat the jurisdiction makes you prove before you're allowed to move in.

Core — call sheetModerate costco_requirements

“What do you require before issuing a certificate of occupancy — final grade, an as-built or final survey, driveway complete, landscaping, address posted, or anything else?”

Certificate of occupancy requirements are the last gate, and anything on that list which isn't in the bid becomes an urgent owner expense at the worst possible moment — when you're paying interest and trying to move in.

Then ask the builderIs everything required for the certificate of occupancy included in your contract?
Got the requirement list

Take the list line by line against your bid and mark anything that isn't clearly covered. Final grade, seeding or stabilization, the driveway surface, and a final survey are the usual gaps. Ask your builder to confirm in writing that every item on the county's list is in their scope — and if any item isn't, get it priced now rather than discovering it during your final week.

Varies by inspector or situation

Ask for whatever written checklist they use, even an informal one, and ask what most commonly holds up a certificate of occupancy in that jurisdiction. That last question often produces the most useful answer of the whole call. Share whatever you learn with your builder and ask how they plan to handle each item.

Nothing beyond passing final inspection

Then your risk here is low, but still confirm with your builder what 'complete' means in their contract — specifically whether final grade, driveway, and cleanup are included, since those are the items most often left informally undefined even where the county doesn't require them.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask this one whenever you next reach the building department — it's quick and it's the cheapest insurance on the list. In the meantime, ask your builder for their own definition of what they deliver at completion, in writing, and compare the two when you get the county's answer.

Core — call sheetModerate costenergy_code_testing

“Which energy code edition do you enforce, and do you require blower door or duct leakage testing by a third party?”

Energy code stringency varies enormously between jurisdictions and drives insulation, window, and mechanical specifications. Required third-party testing is a real line item, and a failed test means rework at the worst point in the schedule.

Then ask the builderDoes your bid include the required energy testing, and were the windows and insulation specified to this jurisdiction's code?
Testing required

Ask who's allowed to perform the test and roughly what it costs, then confirm with your builder that it's in their number and that they've built to pass it. Ask specifically what happens if the house fails — whose cost is the remediation and the retest. Builders working regularly in that jurisdiction will have a ready answer; one who doesn't may not have built to that code before.

No third-party testing required

Still ask which code edition they enforce and pass it to your builder, since the specification requirements apply whether or not testing is required. Then confirm the window, insulation, and mechanical specs in your bid actually meet it. This is a place where a bid priced for a lighter-touch jurisdiction quietly under-specifies.

Depends on the compliance path chosen

Ask what the available compliance paths are and which one your builder intends to use, because they carry different costs — some trade better insulation against testing requirements. Get your builder's chosen path in writing along with confirmation that the bid reflects it. If they haven't decided, that's a sign the mechanical and insulation numbers may be placeholders.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

The building department can answer this quickly. It's less urgent than the site and septic questions, so save it for your second call — but do ask it before signing, because energy code drives insulation and window specifications that are expensive to change after the fact.

ExtendedModerate costengineer_seal

“Do the house plans themselves need an engineer's or architect's seal — for the structure, the foundation, the trusses, or the energy compliance?”

A seal requirement means a licensed professional has to review or stamp the drawings, which is a real cost and can force changes to a stock or designer-drawn plan.

Then ask the builderDo my plans meet this jurisdiction's sealing requirements as drawn, and if not, who pays to get them sealed?
Yes — a seal is required

Ask precisely which portions need sealing, since a truss engineering package and a fully sealed structural set are very different prices. Then ask whoever drew your plans whether they can be sealed as drawn or need revision, and ask your builder who carries that cost. If your plans came from a stock plan service, this is a common gap worth resolving before you pay for permits.

Only certain elements or above a size

Get the specifics and check them against your plans. Trusses, beams, and retaining walls are the usual triggers. Ask your builder whether their number includes any required engineering, and get the answer in writing — it's small relative to the house and annoying to absorb unexpectedly.

No seal required

Confirm that includes trusses, since truss engineering is often supplied by the manufacturer and required even where the plans aren't sealed. Then move on — this one is resolved.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask the plan review desk directly. It's a quick answer and it's worth having before you submit, since discovering a seal requirement at submittal costs you a review cycle as well as the engineering fee.

ExtendedLow costrequired_inspections

“What's your full inspection list for a new home, and are there any inspections here that builders from outside the area tend to miss?”

The second half of that question is the useful part — permit staff know exactly which requirement out-of-area builders forget, and they'll usually tell you.

Then ask the builderHave you built in this jurisdiction before, and are you familiar with their inspection sequence?
Got the list

Skim it for anything unusual and ask your builder about those specifically. A failed or missed inspection means rework and delay, and it's usually a sequencing problem rather than a quality problem. If the county named something out-of-area builders commonly miss, pass that along to your builder directly — it's a genuinely useful thing to hand them.

Standard list, nothing unusual

Fine — note it and move on. Just confirm your builder knows the sequence and who schedules the inspections, since a missed scheduling step costs days at a time when days are expensive.

Minimal inspection requirements

Fewer inspections means less external verification of your builder's work, which raises rather than lowers your interest in a third-party inspection of your own. Consider hiring an independent inspector at framing and before drywall — it's a modest cost relative to what it can catch.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Low priority for cost. Ask it if you're already on the phone, and pay particular attention to the 'what do outside builders miss' question — it's the highest-yield sentence on this whole list and it takes ten seconds.

Flood, fire, overlays & special districtsLocation-triggered requirements that can rewrite the cost of the house.

Core — call sheetVery high costflood_zone

“Is my parcel in a mapped special flood hazard area — and if so, what does that require: an elevation certificate, a minimum lowest-floor elevation, or restrictions on fill?”

A flood zone designation changes the foundation, the fill, the insurance, and sometimes whether the house can sit where the plan puts it. It's determined by the parcel, not the builder, and it's rarely reflected in an early bid.

Then ask the builderDoes your foundation and site number account for the flood zone requirements on this parcel?
Yes — parcel is in a mapped flood hazard area

Ask the county for the required lowest-floor elevation and whether an elevation certificate is required before and after construction, then take both to your builder. This can change the foundation type, the amount of imported fill, and the driveway approach — none of which are minor. Ask your builder to confirm in writing that their foundation and site numbers were priced against that elevation requirement, and get flood insurance quotes before you sign, since the premium is part of your true monthly cost and can be substantial.

Partially — only part of the parcel is affected

Get the flood zone boundary in relation to your actual building footprint, driveway, and septic area, not just the parcel as a whole. A house sited outside the zone can still have a driveway or drain field inside it, and those carry their own requirements. Ask whether the siting on your plan has been checked against the boundary by anyone, and if not, that check should happen before you sign.

No — not in a mapped flood hazard area

Good, and it's still worth asking two follow-ups: whether the maps are currently under revision for your area, and whether the county has any local drainage or wet-weather requirements independent of the federal maps. Some jurisdictions regulate low areas the federal maps don't show. Neither is likely to be expensive, but both are cheap to ask now and costly to discover later.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

This is one you can check yourself for free — FEMA publishes a public flood map lookup by address, and the county floodplain administrator can confirm it. Do this before your next conversation with your builder, because if the answer is yes, several parts of the bid need to be re-examined rather than adjusted.

“Does the fire marshal require residential sprinklers, a minimum fire flow or hydrant distance, or a turnaround for fire apparatus on my driveway?”

Fire requirements are jurisdiction-specific, sit outside the building department, and can add a sprinkler system, a water storage tank, or a widened driveway with a turnaround — any one of which is five figures and none of which appear in a standard bid.

Then ask the builderHave the fire marshal's requirements for this address been checked, and are they in your number?
Yes — one or more fire requirements apply

Get the specific requirement in writing from the fire marshal's office, then take it to your builder and ask for it as a separate line item. Sprinklers, a storage tank or cistern, and a driveway turnaround are three distinct costs and a builder may have caught one and missed the others. If your lot is long or narrow, pay particular attention to the driveway requirement — width, grade, and turning radius specifications can force a redesign, not just a price change.

Depends on distance to a hydrant or house size

Ask for the exact triggering distance or square footage and find out where your house lands against it. This is worth measuring rather than estimating — being just over a hydrant distance threshold is the difference between nothing and a five-figure requirement. If you're close to the line, ask the fire marshal what documentation they'll want and whether siting the house differently changes the answer.

No additional fire requirements

Confirm who told you that — this answer should come from the fire marshal or fire district, not the building permit counter, since they're often separate authorities with separate requirements. Once confirmed, you can stop worrying about it. If a bid line for sprinklers exists anyway, ask whether it can come out.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask the building department who reviews residential plans for fire, then call that office directly. In rural areas this is often a volunteer or district fire department rather than a county office, and they may be reachable only at certain hours. It's worth the effort — this question has one of the widest cost spreads on the list.

“Is my parcel in any overlay or special district — watershed protection, stream buffer, wetlands, historic, design review, scenic corridor, or a wildfire hazard area?”

Overlays sit on top of normal zoning and add requirements that a standard bid never anticipates: buffer setbacks that move the house, design review that changes materials, or wildfire rules that dictate roofing, siding, and vents.

Then ask the builderHas anyone checked whether this parcel falls in an overlay district, and does the bid reflect it?
Yes — one or more overlays apply

Get the name of each overlay and ask the county what it specifically requires for a new single-family home — the requirements vary far too widely to assume. Then take that list to your builder and ask, item by item, which ones their bid accounts for. Pay closest attention to anything that dictates materials or the position of the house, since those change the drawings rather than just the price. If design or architectural review is involved, ask how long that review takes and whether it happens before or after permit application.

Possibly — depends on where the house sits on the lot

Ask for the boundary and compare it against your actual proposed house location, driveway, and septic area. Stream buffers in particular are measured from the water's edge and can consume a surprising amount of a lot. If your siting is close to a boundary, get it verified by whoever prepares your site plan before you sign a contract — moving a house on paper is free and moving it after permitting is not.

No overlays on this parcel

Confirm they checked the parcel specifically rather than the general area, and ask whether any overlay is currently proposed or pending for your area. Once confirmed, this is one you can set aside. If your bid carries allowances tied to special requirements, ask what they were for.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Ask the planning or zoning department rather than the building department — overlays are a zoning question and the permit counter often won't know. Give them your parcel number rather than your address if you have it, since overlays are mapped by parcel. This is usually a quick answer once you reach the right desk.

“Is this lot in a subdivision with recorded covenants or an architectural review committee?”

HOA and covenant review is entirely separate from county approval, can dictate materials, roof pitch, square footage minimums, and siting, and has its own timeline that runs before or alongside permitting.

Then ask the builderHave my plans been submitted to the HOA or architectural review committee, and does the bid reflect any covenant requirements?
Yes — covenants or an ARC apply

Get the recorded covenants and the review committee's submittal requirements yourself rather than relying on a summary — covenants routinely dictate minimum square footage, roof pitch, exterior materials, and even garage orientation, and any one of those can change your cost or your plan. Submit early, since committee approval can take weeks and often runs before the county will act. Then check the covenant requirements line by line against what your builder priced.

Covenants exist but no active committee

Get a copy of the recorded covenants anyway, because they remain enforceable by other lot owners even when no committee is active. Read the material and size requirements specifically and confirm your plans comply. This is cheap to check and unpleasant to litigate.

No covenants on this lot

One less approval path. Note that the county may not be authoritative on this — a title search is the definitive answer, and you'll be getting one anyway. Ask your closing attorney or title company to confirm.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

The county often won't know this. Ask your real estate agent, the seller, or your title company instead, and get a copy of anything recorded against the parcel. If you're buying the lot, this should be resolved before closing rather than before building.

Core — call sheetHigh costcontractor_license

“Does my builder need to be licensed or registered in this jurisdiction to pull the permit — and can you tell me whether they currently are?”

Licensing is jurisdiction-specific, and a builder who isn't registered where you're building either can't pull the permit or has to work through someone who can. It's also the fastest independent check on whether they actually build in this area.

Then ask the builderAre you licensed and registered to pull permits in this jurisdiction, and how many homes have you permitted here?
Yes — and my builder is licensed here

Good. While you have them on the phone, ask whether the permit will be pulled in the builder's name or yours — this matters more than it sounds. A permit pulled in the owner's name can shift responsibility for code compliance and inspections onto you. If your builder proposes an owner-pulled permit, ask why, and get the reasoning in writing before you agree.

Not required / no local registration

Then check the state licensing board separately, since many states license builders at the state level even where counties don't register them. Verify the license is current and in the builder's legal business name, and ask the county whether they've done work in that jurisdiction before. A builder unfamiliar with a jurisdiction isn't disqualifying, but it does mean their permit and site assumptions deserve more scrutiny.

Required, but my builder isn't registered here

Ask your builder directly how they intend to pull the permit and get the answer in writing before you sign anything. If the plan involves pulling it in your name or through another party, understand exactly what that means for who carries responsibility for code compliance, inspections, and any violations. Ask the county what the process and timeline is for a builder to register, since that may simply be the clean answer.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

You can often check this yourself through the state licensing board's online lookup. Search the builder's exact legal business name rather than the name on their sign, since those sometimes differ. Confirm the license is active and note the expiration date.

“How long is the building permit valid, what keeps it active, and what does an extension cost?”

Permits expire, usually on a use-it-or-lose-it basis tied to inspection activity. On a slow or stalled job this becomes a real problem, and it's a useful question to have answered before it matters.

Then ask the builderWhat's your projected completion time, and what happens to the permit if the job runs past it?
Got the validity period and extension terms

Compare the validity period against your builder's schedule and note how much margin exists. If it's tight, ask your builder what their plan is if the job runs long, and who pays for an extension. Also note what keeps a permit active — usually a passed inspection within a set interval — since that matters most if the job stalls.

Depends on inspection activity

Get the interval — how long the permit can go without an inspection before it lapses. That's the number that matters on a slow job. Ask your builder to confirm they'll maintain enough activity to keep it alive, and understand what reinstatement costs if it does lapse.

No expiration / very long validity

One less thing to track. While you're on the subject, ask your builder for their projected completion date in writing and what the contract says if the job runs past it — a generous permit doesn't protect you from an open-ended schedule, and schedule is carrying cost if you're financing.

Didn't ask / couldn't reach them

Lowest priority on the list. Worth knowing eventually, but it only becomes relevant if the job stalls, and it's easy to ask at any point during construction.
